Why it’s true

A regulated utility is a monopoly with captive customers and near-guaranteed cost recovery — one of the lowest-risk businesses in the economy, a fact the market confirms in the low volatility of these stocks (their beta runs far below the market average).

Yet state regulators authorize them shareholder returns of roughly 9.5 to 10 percent — a level sized for companies that face real competitive risk.

The law has required otherwise for a century. In Bluefield (1923) and Hope (1944), the Supreme Court held that a utility’s return must be “commensurate with returns… on investments in other enterprises having corresponding risks.” This tool measures the distance between the two.

Why some large utilities are missing — and why that’s part of the finding. This covers 28 of the roughly 50 largest U.S. investor-owned utilities. Several others are absent because they set rates through “black-box” settlements that never disclose an authorized return at all — among them PECO, PPL Electric, Ameren Missouri, Evergy, Eversource Massachusetts, and Entergy Texas. For millions of customers, the central term of the “regulatory compact” — the profit the utility is guaranteed — is never written where the public can see it. A bargain whose terms are secret cannot be audited or contested. Every total here is therefore an undercount.

How we got it — and how to check it

Risk-justified return = the risk-free rate + (beta × the equity risk premium) — the standard cost-of-equity model used in rate cases. Beta measures a company’s risk; we use the utility sector’s own published figure from NYU’s Aswath Damodaran, re-levered to each utility. Every input below is a slider — move them and the numbers update. The assumptions are conservative, so the gap is, if anything, understated.

The two beta methods — and the tradeoff

Damodaran sector (re-levered). Start from Damodaran’s unlevered beta for the regulated-utility sector — the risk with no debt (0.15) — and re-lever it to each utility’s own capital structure: levered beta = 0.15 × (1 + (1 − 21%) × Debt/Equity). One authoritative source, hard to accuse of cherry-picking. Its weakness: it applies the same sector risk to everyone, so it washes out a specific utility’s elevated risk — including wildfire and climate exposure.

Market beta (per-utility). Use each parent company’s actual market beta. Its strength: it captures the risk the market really perceives for that utility — so wildfire-exposed utilities (Edison ≈0.67, Dominion ≈0.66) get a higher risk-justified return, and their measured over-earning shrinks. Its weakness: betas vary by data provider and window, so a single number is more contestable.

Why keep both. Damodaran is the clean baseline; market beta is the honest answer to “but our climate risk is real.” Even under market betas, most utilities still over-earn.

Does climate change justify a higher return?

The utility’s argument. Climate change makes the grid more expensive to build and riskier to run. Since return should match risk, more risk should mean a higher justified return.

Why it mostly doesn’t hold. Spending more isn’t a reason to earn a higher rate — risk is — and a bigger climate rate base already means bigger profit at a flat return. The genuinely risky parts are narrow and better handled by targeted tools: physical damage is recovered from ratepayers; stranded assets are recovered cheaply through securitization; wildfire liability is concentrated in a few Western states and addressed by wildfire funds and liability reform, not a nationwide return premium.

The market already prices what’s real. If climate had raised utilities’ systematic risk, betas would have risen; the sector’s stays near 0.15 and ratings stay investment-grade. Where the risk is real, the market-beta mode above already gives those utilities a higher justified return — and even then they tend to over-earn.

One honest caveat: authorized vs. earned

This tool measures whether the authorized return exceeds what risk justifies — because rates are built on the authorized return, that gap is what lands on bills. Whether a utility then earns more or less than its authorized return is a separate performance question: some beat it, several here earn less. That variance doesn’t change the finding. The over-earning measured here is baked into the rates customers pay, regardless of how the utility performs against it.

This is the evidence. Here’s the case.

The “regulatory compact” — the idea that utilities are owed a protected profit — isn’t law; it’s a story. It’s time to give it real content: a return matched to risk, and an energy bill of rights that finally covers what you pay, not just how you’re treated.
