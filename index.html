Un-Risky Business: How much more do utilities earn than their risk justifies?

There is already a large body of literature affirming utilities are “overearning,” alongside a body of work challenging that conclusion. Settling the debate requires an objective benchmark and the law provides one: a utility’s return is judged against its risk.

Read the legal basis for the claim: the law requires a return matched to its risk

Utility ratemaking is ruled by a legal standard: utilities make large capital investments in the grid, and through regulated rates, receive a reasonable opportunity to recover prudently incurred costs plus earn a reasonable return. In other words, a reasonable return commensurate with the risk. The Supreme Court affirmed this on two occasions:

Hope (1944): FPC v. Hope Natural Gas Co., 320 U.S. 591, 603: “…the return to the equity owner should be commensurate with returns on investments in other enterprises having corresponding risks. That return, moreover, should be sufficient to assure confidence in the financial integrity of the enterprise, so as to maintain its credit and to attract capital.”
Bluefield (1923): Bluefield Water Works v. PSC, 262 U.S. 679, 692–93: “A public utility is entitled to such rates as will permit it to earn a return… equal to that generally being made at the same time and in the same general part of the country on investments in other business undertakings which are attended by corresponding risks and uncertainties, but it has no constitutional right to profits such as are realized or anticipated in highly profitable enterprises or speculative ventures.”

At the same time, the Federal Power Act of 1935, §205 (16 U.S.C. §824d(a)) states that electricity rates “shall be just and reasonable, and any such rate or charge that is not just and reasonable is hereby declared to be unlawful.”

The job of the regulator then is to set an overall rate that is “just and reasonable.” One component of that standard is a return on the utility’s investment that offers a “reasonable return.” Nothing in the Constitution puts a ceiling on returns. However, the law does not guarantee utilities profit or the right to rich returns. What it protects is only the floor:

Chief Justice Rehnquist in Duquesne (488 U.S. at 307–08): “the Constitution protects utilities from being limited to a charge for their property serving the public which is so ‘unjust’ as to be confiscatory.”

But the statutory “just and reasonable” standard does set that ceiling. And the Supreme Court defined a reasonable return as one “commensurate with” returns on investments “having corresponding risks.” Because the law measures a reasonable return against risk, you cannot judge whether a return is reasonable without measuring the risk.

Methodology

Risk-justified return = the risk-free rate + (beta × the equity risk premium): the standard cost-of-equity model used in rate cases. Beta measures a company’s risk; we use the utility sector’s own published figure from NYU’s Aswath Damodaran, re-levered to each utility. The assumptions are conservative, so the gap is, if anything, understated.

The two beta methods, and the tradeoff

Damodaran sector (re-levered). Start from Damodaran’s unlevered beta for the regulated-utility sector (the risk with no debt, 0.15), and re-lever it to each utility’s own capital structure: levered beta = 0.15 × (1 + (1 − 21%) × Debt/Equity). One authoritative source, hard to accuse of cherry-picking. Its weakness: it applies the same sector risk to everyone, so it washes out a specific utility’s elevated risk, including wildfire and climate exposure.

Market beta (per-utility). Use each parent company’s actual market beta. Its strength: it captures the risk the market really perceives for that utility, so wildfire-exposed utilities (Edison ≈0.67, Dominion ≈0.66) get a higher risk-justified return, and their measured over-earning shrinks. Its weakness: betas vary by data provider and window, so a single number is more contestable.

Why keep both. Damodaran is the clean baseline; market beta is the honest answer to “but our climate risk is real.” Even under market betas, most utilities still over-earn.

One honest caveat: authorized vs. earned

This tool measures whether the authorized return exceeds what risk justifies, because rates are built on the authorized return, that gap is what lands on bills. Whether a utility then earns more or less than its authorized return is a separate performance question: some beat it, several here earn less. That variance doesn’t change the finding. The over-earning measured here is baked into the rates customers pay, regardless of how the utility performs against it.
