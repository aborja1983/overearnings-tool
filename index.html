Is your utility over-earning?

What the law says a utility should earn for its risk — versus what it's allowed to charge you.

The governing legal standard (Bluefield 1923, Hope 1944) says a utility's return should match the risk it actually bears. These are monopolies with captive customers — unusually low-risk — yet regulators grant returns set for a far riskier business. This tool measures the gap, and what it costs. Investor-owned utilities only (municipals and co-ops don't earn a set return). Every figure is sourced; the numbers move when you change the assumptions.

First, the three numbers this tool compares. A utility earns a profit rate — its return on equity (ROE) — on the shareholder money invested in the grid. There are three versions of it:
  • Authorized ROE — the profit rate the state regulator allows the utility to charge for. (Usually ~9.5–10%.)
  • Risk-justified ROE — what that profit rate should be, given how little risk a monopoly with captive customers actually runs. This is the standard the law sets (Bluefield/Hope): match the return to the risk.
  • Earned ROE — what the utility actually made.
So the two views below: Authorized vs. risk-justified asks whether the allowed profit is higher than the utility's low risk deserves — that's the core question, and the gap is the "over-earning." Earned vs. authorized asks whether the utility collected even more than it was allowed. New to this? Open "How this works" lower down for the 90-second version.

Risk-justified ROE = risk-free rate + (beta × equity risk premium). Because utilities are low-risk (a beta far below the market's 1.0), the return the law says they deserve is low — that's the number this tool checks the authorized return against.
The two beta methods — and the tradeoff (important)

Damodaran sector (re-levered). Start from Damodaran's unlevered beta for the regulated-utility sector — the risk with no debt (0.15) — and re-lever it to each utility's own capital structure: levered beta = 0.15 × (1 + (1 − 21%) × Debt/Equity). One authoritative source, hard to accuse of cherry-picking. Its weakness: it applies the same sector risk to everyone, so it washes out a specific utility's elevated risk — including wildfire/climate exposure. A California utility that the market treats as genuinely riskier looks identical to a low-risk Midwest one.

Market beta (per-utility). Use each parent company's actual market beta (from stock-return data). Its strength: it captures the risk the market really perceives for that utility — so wildfire-exposed utilities (Edison ~0.67, Dominion ~0.66) get a higher risk-justified return, and their measured over-earning shrinks accordingly. Its weakness: betas vary by data provider and window, so a single number is more contestable.

Why keep both. Flip between them: Damodaran is the clean baseline; market beta is the honest answer to "but our climate risk is real." Even under market betas — which credit that risk — most utilities still over-earn. See the climate question below.

Does climate change justify a higher return?

The utility's argument. Climate change makes the grid more expensive (hardening, undergrounding, transition) and riskier (wildfire liability, disasters, stranded assets). Since return should match risk, a higher risk means a higher justified return.

Why it mostly doesn't hold. Spending more isn't a reason to earn a higher rate — risk is; and a bigger climate rate base already means bigger profit even at a flat return. The genuinely risky parts are narrow and better handled by targeted tools than a blanket premium: physical damage is recovered from ratepayers (so it isn't shareholder risk); stranded assets are recovered cheaply via securitization (which removes the risk from shareholders); wildfire liability is real but concentrated (a few Western states) and addressed by wildfire funds, liability reform, and safety-conditioned recovery — not a nationwide ROE bump. A climate premium also rewards building more capital (the Averch–Johnson bias), tilting utilities toward the most expensive decarbonization, not the most effective.

And the market already prices what's real. If climate had raised utilities' systematic risk, betas would have risen; the sector's stays near 0.15 and ratings stay investment-grade. Where the risk is real (California wildfire), the market-beta mode above already gives those utilities a higher justified return — and even then they tend to over-earn. So the honest position isn't "climate never justifies a cent"; it's match the return to the specific, market-measured risk, and use targeted mechanisms for the tail — not a blanket premium households pay on top of the climate bill.

Coverage — and what's missing is the point. This tool covers 28 of the roughly 50 largest U.S. investor-owned utilities. Most of the rest are absent because their data isn't public — and for several large utilities the missing piece is the authorized ROE itself: they set rates through "black-box" settlements that never disclose the allowed return (for example PECO, PPL Electric, Ameren Missouri, Evergy, Eversource Massachusetts, Entergy Texas). That isn't a footnote — it's evidence. For millions of customers, the central term of the "regulatory compact" — the profit the utility is guaranteed — is never written down where the public can see it. You cannot audit, contest, or hold to account a bargain whose terms are secret. It also means every total here is an undercount: the utilities that keep their returns hidden are exactly the ones you'd most want to check.

  • Not every dollar earns the retail equity premium. Some of the buildout is FERC-jurisdictional transmission (a different ROE), and some is securitized or customer-funded (no equity return at all). How this works (plain language)

A utility is a monopoly. One company owns the poles and wires; you can't shop for another. So a state commission sets what it can charge.

The regulatory compact. The utility invests billions in the grid; in return, regulators let it recover its costs and earn a profit. This so-called "regulatory compact" is where the fight over how much profit is fair plays out.

Rate base & ROE. The utility earns a profit rate — the return on equity, or ROE — on the shareholder money invested in the grid (its "rate base"). Roughly: yearly profit ≈ equity share of rate base × ROE.

Beta. How risky the market thinks a company is (the whole market = 1.0). Utilities are very low-risk. The tool offers two ways to set it — a debt-free sector figure from Damodaran re-levered per utility, or each utility's own market beta — so a low-risk utility earns a low justified return.

The catch. A monopoly with captive customers is unusually safe, and safe should mean low returns. This tool measures the gap between the return utilities are granted and what their real, low risk would justify.

The gap this tool measures is the "regulatory compact" doing its real work. The compact is described as a settled deal in which utilities are owed a protected profit — but that entitlement isn't law. Courts have never guaranteed a profit, and the phrase appears in no statute. What the law actually requires (Bluefield 1923, Hope 1944) is a return matched to risk — and these monopolies are among the safest businesses there are. The premium you see here is that fiction, priced in dollars on your bill.

A real compact would give the metaphor enforceable content. Three reforms follow:

  • Make ratepayer protection a central, enforceable obligation. Today it's an afterthought fought case by case; it should be written into law, not left to discretion.
  • Put the risk on whoever causes it. Bind large new loads — data centers — to enforceable terms so they pay for the upgrades they trigger, keeping the utility's risk low rather than socializing it onto households.
  • Recalibrate the return to the real risk. This is what the tool measures. If a utility bears little risk, its return should be low — exactly what Bluefield/Hope already require, and what the fiction lets utilities escape.

The usual objection — cut returns and capital flees — doesn't hold. High returns on equity have nothing to do with raising debt; the market already prices these utilities as nearly risk-free; capital is pouring in at today's returns; and the buildout can be financed with far cheaper tools (securitization, third-party capital) than an ROE premium ratepayers pay forever.

Caveats. Risk-free rate, equity risk premium, and beta are adjustable. Beta is either Damodaran's (NYU Stern, Jan 2026) unlevered utility beta 0.15 re-levered to each utility's capital structure (tax 21%), or each parent's market beta. Rate-base figures vary by vintage/jurisdiction; Georgia Power's ~$29B is an estimate (FY2024, triangulated). Earned ROE for PG&E (~9.7%) and SCE (~11.6%) is the core, utility-level 2024 figure excluding wildfire charges, computed on book equity. "Over-earning" here means authorized return above the risk-justified return (the Bluefield/Hope test), not that utilities always collect more than allowed.
