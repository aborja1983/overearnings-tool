Is your utility over-earning?

What the law says a utility should earn for its risk — versus what it's allowed to charge you.

The governing legal standard (Bluefield 1923, Hope 1944) says a utility's return should match the risk it actually bears. These are monopolies with captive customers — unusually low-risk — yet regulators grant returns set for a far riskier business. This tool measures the gap, and what it costs. Investor-owned utilities only (municipals and co-ops don't earn a set return). Every figure is sourced; the numbers move when you change the assumptions.

Risk-free rate (10-yr Treasury):
What it is: what a totally safe U.S. government bond pays — the floor. Update from FRED DGS10.
Equity risk premium:
What it is: the extra return investors expect for owning stocks over safe bonds.
Utility-sector beta:
What it is: how risky the market says the sector is (whole market = 1.0). Default is Damodaran's regulated Utility (General) beta, 0.24 (Jan 2026). Slide up to his broader Power category (0.48) to be more conservative.
Putting it together: Risk-justified ROE = risk-free rate + (sector beta × equity risk premium). Because utilities are low-risk (a beta far below the market's 1.0), the return the law says they deserve is low — that's the number this tool checks the authorized return against. Same sector beta is applied to every utility, so the differences you see come from their authorized returns, equity ratios, and size.
How this works (plain language)

A utility is a monopoly. One company owns the poles and wires; you can't shop for another. So a state commission sets what it can charge.

The bargain. The utility invests billions in the grid; in return, regulators let it recover its costs and earn a profit. The fight is over how much profit is fair.

Rate base & ROE. The utility earns a profit rate — the return on equity, or ROE — on the shareholder money invested in the grid (its "rate base"). Roughly: yearly profit ≈ equity share of rate base × ROE.

Why it's the battleground. ROE is a judgment call, usually ~9.5–10%. Because it multiplies a multibillion-dollar rate base, a tenth of a percent is millions of dollars a year — on your bill.

The three slider terms. Risk-free rate = what a safe government bond pays (the floor). Beta = how risky the market thinks the sector is (whole market = 1.0; the regulated-utility sector sits near 0.24, i.e. very safe). Equity risk premium = the extra return investors want for stocks over bonds. A "risk-justified" return = the floor plus beta times that premium — so a low-risk sector earns a low justified return.

The catch. A monopoly with captive customers is unusually safe, and safe should mean low returns. This tool measures the gap between the return utilities are granted and what their real, low risk would justify.

Caveats. Risk-free rate, equity risk premium, and sector beta are adjustable assumptions. Beta is the regulated-utility sector beta from Damodaran (NYU Stern), Jan 2026 — "Utility (General)" 0.24; his broader "Power" category (0.48) is a more conservative choice, applied sector-wide to all utilities. Rate-base figures vary by vintage/jurisdiction; Georgia Power's ~$29B is an estimate (FY2024, triangulated from FERC Form 1 plant growth + Vogtle additions; exact figure pending its 2024 Annual Retail Surveillance Report). Earned ROE for PG&E (~9.7%) and SCE (~11.6%) is the core, utility-level 2024 figure excluding wildfire charges, computed on book equity — it can differ from the regulatory recorded ROE. "Over-earning" here means authorized return above the risk-justified return (the Bluefield/Hope test), not that utilities always collect more than allowed.
