Is your utility over-earning?

What the law says a utility should earn for its risk — versus what it's allowed to charge you.

The governing legal standard (Bluefield 1923, Hope 1944) says a utility's return should match the risk it actually bears. These are monopolies with captive customers — unusually low-risk — yet regulators grant returns set for a far riskier business. This tool measures the gap, and what it costs. Investor-owned utilities only (municipals and co-ops don't earn a set return). Every figure is sourced; the numbers move when you change the assumptions.

Risk-free rate (10-yr Treasury):
What it is: what a totally safe investment — a U.S. government bond — pays. It's the floor every return builds on. Update from FRED DGS10.
Equity risk premium:
What it is: the extra return investors expect for buying stocks instead of safe bonds. A standard published estimate — utilities will argue this number.
Putting it together: Risk-justified ROE = risk-free rate + (the utility's beta × equity risk premium). Beta is how risky the market thinks a company is: the whole stock market scores 1.0; a beta of 0.30 means the stock is about a third as volatile — very safe. Utilities have low betas, so the market itself says they deserve low returns. That's the number this tool checks the authorized return against.
How this works (plain language)

A utility is a monopoly. One company owns the poles and wires; you can't shop for another. So a state commission sets what it can charge.

The bargain. The utility invests billions in the grid; in return, regulators let it recover its costs and earn a profit. The fight is over how much profit is fair.

Rate base & ROE. The utility earns a profit rate — the return on equity, or ROE — on the shareholder money invested in the grid (its "rate base"). Roughly: yearly profit ≈ equity share of rate base × ROE.

Why it's the battleground. ROE is a judgment call, usually ~9.5–10%. Because it multiplies a multibillion-dollar rate base, a tenth of a percent is millions of dollars a year — on your bill.

The catch. A monopoly with captive customers is unusually safe, and safe should mean low returns. This tool measures the gap between the return utilities are granted and what their real, low risk would justify.

The three terms in the sliders. Risk-free rate = what a totally safe U.S. government bond pays (the floor). Beta = how risky the market thinks a company is (the whole market = 1.0; utilities run ~0.3–0.7, i.e. safer than average). Equity risk premium = the extra return investors want for owning stocks over safe bonds. A "risk-justified" return = the safe floor plus a company's risk (beta) times that premium — so a low-risk utility earns a low justified return.

Caveats. Risk-free rate and equity risk premium are adjustable assumptions (defaults are placeholders). Betas are parent-company, point-in-time. Rate-base figures vary by vintage/jurisdiction; Georgia Power's is pre-Vogtle and conservative. PG&E and SCE earned-ROE are omitted (only non-comparable parent-GAAP figures available). "Over-earning" here means authorized return above the risk-justified return — the Bluefield/Hope test — not that utilities always collect more than allowed (the earned-vs-authorized view shows several under-earn their authorized ROE).
