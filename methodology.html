Methodology & Sources

How the numbers are built, and where every figure comes from. Updated August 2026.

Where the data is

Every figure is public and traceable, in three places with identical numbers:

  • The dataset — utility_dataset.csv. One row per utility: rate base, equity ratio, authorized ROE, betas, and notes.
  • The citations — sources.md. Each rate base, ROE, equity ratio, and customer count links to its origin (rate case order, commission filing, or SEC filing).
  • Inside the tool — the “Every figure is sourced” panel and the “Download data (CSV)” button.

The formula (per utility)

  1. Risk-justified ROE = risk-free rate + (beta × equity risk premium)
  2. Gap = authorized ROE − risk-justified ROE
  3. Equity rate base = rate base × equity ratio
  4. Shareholder windfall (after-tax) = gap × equity rate base
  5. Bill impact (pre-tax) = windfall ÷ (1 − corporate tax rate)
  6. Per customer = bill impact ÷ number of customers

Default assumptions (all adjustable in the tool): risk-free rate 4.3% (FRED DGS10); equity risk premium 5.0%; corporate tax 21%; beta = Damodaran’s unlevered utility beta 0.15 (NYU Stern, Jan 2026) re-levered to each utility’s capital structure, or each parent’s market beta.

Worked example — Pacific Gas & Electric. Rate base $69.0B × 52% equity = $35.9B equity rate base. Authorized ROE 9.98% − risk-justified 5.6% = 4.38-point gap. Windfall = 4.38% × $35.9B = $1.57B after-tax; ÷ (1 − 21%) = $1.99B on bills; ÷ 5.5M customers = $362 / customer / year.

FAQ

Where did the rate base numbers come from?

Each utility’s rate base is taken from its own rate case or commission order (or, where noted, its latest SEC/investor filing) — the regulator-set figure, net plant in service plus allowed additions, not our estimate, except the few flagged below. See sources.md for the link behind each one.

Where does the equity ratio (% equity) come from?

It is the authorized capital structure — the share of rate base a utility may finance with shareholder equity rather than debt — set by the commission in the same rate case order that sets the authorized ROE. Each is cited in sources.md. It matters because a utility earns its ROE only on the equity portion of rate base; the debt portion earns interest, not the shareholder return. So the equity ratio determines how much of rate base earns the ROE. In this dataset it runs from about 48% (Con Edison) to 59.6% (Florida Power & Light), most around 50–53%. Utilities argue for higher equity ratios precisely because more equity means more of the rate base earns the higher ROE instead of cheaper debt — a related lever to the ROE itself.

You can only rate-base capex, not operating costs — did you account for that?

Yes, by construction. The return premium is applied only to the equity share of rate base (step 3 above) — never to revenue, O&M, or total spending. Rate base is capital investment (net plant), so operating costs never enter the calculation. We also use each utility’s regulatory rate base (net of accumulated depreciation), not gross capex, which is the more conservative figure.

What about the $1.4 trillion buildout number — isn’t that gross capex?

That is the one place gross capex appears, and it is handled separately. The buildout figure is five-year gross capex, which overstates net rate-base additions. The tool’s buildout calculator discounts it with a “share earning the premium” slider (default 80%), which removes FERC-jurisdictional, securitized, and customer-funded dollars and absorbs the gross-to-net gap. It is labeled a scoping estimate, not a precise forecast.

Which figures are estimates rather than filed numbers?

Three are triangulated and flagged in the notes: Georgia Power’s rate base (~$29B, post-Vogtle), Duke Energy Florida’s rate base (2024 black-box settlement), and Dominion Energy Virginia’s Virginia-jurisdiction base. Everything else is a filed figure.

Why only 28 utilities?

Several large utilities set rates through “black-box” settlements that never disclose an authorized ROE (PECO, PPL Electric, Ameren Missouri, Evergy, Eversource Massachusetts, Entergy Texas, and others). They can’t be included without a disclosed return — which is itself part of the finding. Every total is therefore an undercount.

Legal standard being tested

Bluefield Waterworks v. PSC, 262 U.S. 679 (1923), and FPC v. Hope Natural Gas, 320 U.S. 591 (1944): a utility’s return must be “commensurate with returns on investments in other enterprises having corresponding risks.” The tool measures the distance between the authorized return and that risk-justified benchmark.
